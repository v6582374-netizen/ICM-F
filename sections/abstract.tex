Generative AI is rapidly transforming the task composition of occupations across all sectors. We develop a data-driven \textbf{Task DNA} framework to quantify how Gen-AI reshapes occupational structures and to inform curriculum decisions at post-secondary institutions.

\textbf{Problem and Approach.} We select three representative occupations---one each from STEM, Trade, and Arts---by maximizing a heterogeneity measure in a three-dimensional feature space (digitization, physical anchoring, IP-risk) derived from O*NET descriptors. This geometric optimization yields \emph{Robotics Engineers}, \emph{First-Line Supervisors of Mechanics}, and \emph{Court Reporters} as the maximally diverse triplet (triangle area = 0.262).

\textbf{Model.} For each occupation we construct a 20-task DNA profile weighted by importance and frequency. We assign substitution and complementarity scores ($s_{ij}, c_{ij}$) to each task based on a nine-dimension Gen-AI capability scale, then simulate task-share evolution under three adoption trajectories $A(t)$. Key outputs include the task-level net impact $\mu_{ij}^{task}=c_{ij}-s_{ij}$, structural flip intensity, and an effective vanishing time $\tau_{ij}(\epsilon)$.

\textbf{Results.} Court Reporters face the highest substitution pressure ($\alpha=0.68$), while Robotics Engineers exhibit strong complementarity ($\beta=0.71$). Sensitivity analysis shows that a 20\% increase in adoption rate accelerates task restructuring by approximately 1.5 years.

\textbf{Policy Implications.} Based on model outputs, we recommend program-specific strategies: expand robotics curricula with Gen-AI co-design modules; retool trade programs toward predictive-maintenance diagnostics; and pivot court-reporting programs toward real-time AI-assisted captioning.
