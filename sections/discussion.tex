\section{Discussion: Strengths and Limitations}
\subsection{Strengths}

\paragraph{First Principles: Occupations Are Composed of Tasks}
Our modeling approach stems from a simple first principle---\textbf{the essence of an occupation is its task composition}. Since GenAI impacts specific tasks rather than abstract ``occupation labels,'' studying occupational evolution should start from the task level. Existing empirical evidence also indicates that GenAI adoption is reshaping work content and skill demand structures, and this change first manifests in the task and skill composition within positions, rather than simply equating to ``a certain occupation being substituted/not substituted''~\citep{gulati2025generativeaiadopti}. Based on this, we propose the concept of \textbf{Task DNA}, decomposing each occupation into a measurable task weight vector, making the vague question of ``AI's impact on occupations'' computable and traceable.

\paragraph{Innovation: From ``Is This Occupation at Risk?'' to ``How Are Tasks Being Rearranged?''}
Traditional research often provides vague conclusions like ``Occupation A is high-risk / Occupation B is safe.'' Our model answers more granular questions: which task shares will contract, which will expand, and when occupational boundaries trigger redefinition. This task-level granularity of analysis aligns with research conclusions in education and labor studies that ``career readiness needs to be defined by human-machine complementary skills rather than static job labels,'' thus having greater interpretability and policy guidance value~\citep{mughal2025beyondautomationho,wong2025thefutureoflearnin}.

\subsection{Limitations}
Due to the short development period of Gen-AI, we lack sufficient time-series data to demonstrate intuitive structural mutations.
Furthermore, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:task_share_all_stem}, the magnitude of change in the structural curves is very small, resulting in effects that are not visually pronounced.
