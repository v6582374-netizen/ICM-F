\subsection{Career Selection as a Modeled Decision}

A prerequisite of the problem requires selecting one career each from the STEM, Trade, and Arts sectors. 
Rather than treating this step as an ad hoc assumption, we explicitly model career selection as an optimization problem.
This ensures that subsequent analyses of AI-driven impacts and educational recommendations are grounded in a reproducible and data-informed framework.

\subsubsection{Modeling Rationale}

The core objective of career selection is not representativeness of all occupations, but \emph{explanatory contrast}.
Specifically, the selected careers should differ substantially in how generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) interacts with their task structures, while remaining empirically tractable and policy-relevant.

Based on the problem statement and data availability constraints, we identify three dominant dimensions that govern heterogeneity in AI impact across occupations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digitizability}: the extent to which core tasks can be represented, automated, or augmented digitally;
    \item \textbf{Physical anchoring}: the degree to which task execution requires on-site physical presence or manual interaction with the environment;
    \item \textbf{Intellectual property and attribution risk}: the sensitivity of value creation to authorship, originality, and ownership.
\end{itemize}

Each candidate career is represented by a normalized feature vector
\begin{equation}
    f(x)=\big(digit(x),\,physical(x),\,iprisk(x)\big)\in[0,1]^3,
\end{equation}
where higher values indicate stronger presence of the corresponding characteristic.

In addition to mechanistic contrast, the problem explicitly emphasizes data-informed reasoning.
Therefore, we introduce a data availability score $D(x)$ that reflects whether reliable data sources exist for employment trends, task descriptions, compensation, and educational pathways associated with career $x$.
These data sources include occupational databases \cite{onet2025database}, post-secondary employment outcomes \cite{censuspseo2025}, educational institutional data \cite{ncesipeds2018}, and occupational classification crosswalks \cite{ncescip2020soc2018}.

\subsubsection{Objectification of Career Feature Parameters Based on O*NET}

To avoid subjective assignment of the career feature parameters $\textit{digit}(x)$, $\textit{physical}(x)$, and $\textit{iprisk}(x)$, we adopt the \textbf{O*NET} occupational database maintained by the U.S. Department of Labor as a unified data source, with detailed data provenance summarized in Section 3.
O*NET provides a large number of \emph{numerical scale descriptors} based on expert surveys and practitioner feedback for each occupation, with most indicators naturally given on a continuous scale of $0$--$100$, suitable for direct use in quantitative modeling.

\paragraph{Parameter Construction Logic}
We do not attempt to find the ``unique correct'' indicator for each dimension, but rather follow the \emph{shortest defensible path} principle: for each feature axis, we select a small number of semantically directly corresponding and downloadable O*NET descriptors and use them as proxy variables for that axis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digitizability $\textit{digit}(x)$}  
    This dimension characterizes the extent to which occupational tasks rely on computers and electronic information flows. We select work activity and work context descriptors from O*NET that are directly related to ``working with computers'' and ``frequency of electronic communication''. The higher the value, the more the occupation is centered on digital media.

    \item \textbf{Physical anchoring $\textit{physical}(x)$}  
    This dimension characterizes the degree to which an occupation depends on on-site operations, manual control, and physical posture constraints. We use work context descriptors that reflect ``time spent performing hands-on activities'' and ``physical posture burdens such as kneeling, squatting, or bending'', to quantify whether the occupation requires physical presence and physical operations.

    \item \textbf{Intellectual property / attribution risk $\textit{iprisk}(x)$ (proxy indicator)}  
    This dimension does not directly measure the probability of legal litigation, but rather serves as a \emph{proxy indicator} to characterize the dependence of occupational output on original expression and creative contribution. We select ability and work activity descriptors related to ``Originality'' and ``Thinking Creatively'' from O*NET. The higher the intensity of original expression, the more likely it is to trigger copyright, attribution, or style attribution disputes when generative models intervene.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Normalization and Reproducibility}
For each occupation $x$, let $v_m(x)$ denote its raw rating ($0$--$100$) under O*NET descriptor $m$.
We apply min-max normalization across the entire occupation set:
\begin{equation}
    z_m(x)=\frac{v_m(x)-\min_{y}v_m(y)}{\max_{y}v_m(y)-\min_{y}v_m(y)} \in [0,1],
\end{equation}
and take the equally weighted average of multiple descriptors under the same feature axis to obtain $\textit{digit}(x)$, $\textit{physical}(x)$, and $\textit{iprisk}(x)$.
This process is entirely based on publicly available data and explicit rules, ensuring the reproducibility and auditability of parameter values.

\subsubsection{Optimization Formulation}

For a triplet of careers $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ drawn respectively from STEM, Trade, and Arts categories, we define the pairwise contrast between two careers as the Euclidean distance between their feature vectors:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta(x_i,x_j)=\lVert f(x_i)-f(x_j)\rVert_2.
\end{equation}

The overall selection objective is then formulated as
\begin{equation}
    \max_{x_1\in STEM,\;x_2\in Trade,\;x_3\in Arts}
    \left(
    \sum_{i<j}\Delta(x_i,x_j)
    +\lambda\sum_{k=1}^{3}D(x_k)
    \right),
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ balances explanatory contrast against empirical feasibility.

This formulation does not claim global optimality across all possible occupations.
Instead, it yields a deliberately constructed high-contrast career set that maximizes analytical clarity under realistic data and time constraints.

\subsubsection{Revision and Final Configuration of Selected Careers}

In the initial scheme, we selected Structural/Civil Engineer as the STEM occupation. However, upon further examination of the distribution of the three occupations across the ``task structure'' and ``physical constraint'' dimensions, we found that this choice exhibits some similarity with the Trade occupation (Electrician) along the ``physical anchoring strength'' axis, potentially weakening the contrast among the three occupations in the mechanism space.

To maximize task structure heterogeneity and enhance the identifiability of subsequent GenAI impact mechanisms, we revise the STEM endpoint by replacing it with \textbf{Software Developer / Software Engineer}.

This revision does not alter the structure of the original selection model, but merely adjusts the position of the occupational endpoint in the feature space: software developers possess extremely high digitizability and extremely low physical anchoring, thereby significantly increasing their distance from electricians in the feature vector $f(x)$ and improving the value of the overall contrast objective function.

It must be emphasized that we do not assume that the software development occupation will be simply ``replaced'' by GenAI. On the contrary, this occupation is more likely to undergo \textbf{task structure reorganization}: the proportion of coding and boilerplate implementation decreases, while the proportion of tasks such as requirement clarification, system architecture design, verification testing, and security and compliance responsibilities increases. It is precisely this shift from ``execution-oriented tasks'' to ``verification and responsibility-oriented tasks'' that makes software development an ideal STEM endpoint for studying GenAI impact mechanisms.

After completing this revision, the final configuration of the three selected occupations is as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{STEM: Software Developer / Software Engineer.}  
    High digitizability, low physical anchoring; GenAI can directly intervene in core tasks, but primarily manifests as task redistribution rather than occupational extinction.

    \item \textbf{Trade: Electrician.}  
    High physical anchoring, strong on-site constraints; AI primarily serves as a planning, diagnostic, and auxiliary tool, difficult to replace core operational tasks.

    \item \textbf{Arts: Commercial Illustrator / Graphic Designer.}  
    High intellectual property and attribution sensitivity; GenAI directly affects output forms and value pricing mechanisms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model Selection for Occupational Evolution}

The second core requirement of the problem is to construct a \emph{data-informed} model of future occupational evolution.
Before introducing formal equations, we first define what ``future evolution'' means in computable terms and then systematically evaluate alternative modeling approaches, so that model choice itself is not an implicit assumption.

\subsubsection{Computable Definition of Future Evolution}

In this paper, the future evolution of occupation $i$ is characterized by two primary outputs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Occupational demand}: the time evolution of job demand $E_i(t)$ over a 10-year horizon, under fast, medium, and slow GenAI adoption scenarios;
    \item \textbf{Task structure}: the reallocation of task weights $T_i(t)$ within the occupation, indicating which tasks expand, contract, or are reorganized.
\end{itemize}

This definition ensures that occupational-level outcomes are derived from task-level mechanisms rather than imposed directly.

\subsubsection{Comparison of Candidate Modeling Approaches}

We consider three representative approaches for modeling occupational evolution:

\paragraph{Approach A: Task-aggregate impact model}
This approach treats an occupation as a weighted bundle of tasks.
GenAI-driven substitution and augmentation effects are specified at the task level and then aggregated to the occupational level using task weights.
Most parameters can be constructed from O*NET task data \cite{onet2025database} and publicly available AI exposure rubrics \cite{freyosborne2017}, providing transparency and reproducibility.

\paragraph{Approach B: Job-posting time-series models}
This approach infers occupational change from trends in AI-related keywords and skill requirements in job postings.
While closely tied to market signals, it suffers from identification challenges and high data-processing overhead, making it unsuitable as the main modeling backbone.

\paragraph{Approach C: Agent-based simulations}
Agent-based models simulate strategic interactions among firms, workers, and educational institutions.
Despite their narrative richness, they require extensive calibration and introduce substantial model complexity, which conflicts with transparency requirements under strict page limits.

\subsubsection{Final Model Choice}

Based on the above comparison, we adopt the task-aggregate impact model (Approach A) as our primary framework.
This choice is motivated by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanistic clarity}: occupational change emerges explicitly from task-level effects;
    \item \textbf{Data alignment}: key parameters are grounded in publicly available occupational data \cite{onet2025database,censuspseo2025};
    \item \textbf{Controlled complexity}: the model remains interpretable and suitable for scenario and sensitivity analysis;
    \item \textbf{Transferability}: the framework can be readily extended to other occupations or educational pathways \cite{ncescip2020soc2018}.
\end{itemize}

Approach B is reserved for potential validation or supplementary analysis, while Approach C is excluded from the main analysis due to calibration and transparency concerns.

\subsection{Task-Aggregate Dynamic Model of Occupational Evolution}

Having justified the task-aggregate approach, we now present its mathematical formulation.
We adopt a minimal task-to-occupation framework: GenAI adoption $A(t)$ enters as an exogenous driver, substitution and augmentation are specified at the task level, and occupation-level net impact is derived by task-weight aggregation, yielding a dynamic trajectory for occupational demand $E_i(t)$.

\subsubsection{Task Aggregation and Occupational Dynamics}

Let occupation $i$ consist of tasks $j=1,\dots,J_i$ with weights $w_{ij}$ such that
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=1}^{J_i} w_{ij} = 1.
\end{equation}
For each task $j$, let $s_{ij}\in[0,1]$ denote substitution intensity and $c_{ij}\in[0,1]$ denote augmentation (complementarity) intensity.
Define occupation-level aggregate effects as
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_i=\sum_{j=1}^{J_i} w_{ij}\,s_{ij},\qquad
    \beta_i=\sum_{j=1}^{J_i} w_{ij}\,c_{ij}.
\end{equation}

Let $E_i(t)$ be employment demand for occupation $i$ at time $t$, and let $g_i$ capture baseline growth/decay from non-AI factors (either estimated from public trends or set to $0$ under scenario analysis).
Given a scenario family of adoption trajectories $A(t)\in[0,1]$ (fast/medium/slow), we model occupational evolution as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dE_i(t)}{dt}=E_i(t)\Big(g_i + A(t)\,(\beta_i-\alpha_i)\Big).
\end{equation}
This formulation ensures that occupational outcomes are derived from task-level mechanisms, supporting interpretability, reproducibility, and data-informed reasoning.

\subsection{GenAI Capability-Dimension Scoring from Authoritative Reports: Scale Definition and Evidence Narrative}

To replace the subjective ``keyword tagging'' method, we adopt capability ratings derived from authoritative evaluations and official technical reports.
These ratings quantify GenAI \textbf{substitution} and \textbf{augmentation} capabilities across major software-development task dimensions, providing auditable grounds for mapping Software Developer Task DNA to $(s,c)$ inputs.

\subsubsection{Scale Definition}

We use three levels (Low/Medium/High) to represent GenAI capability, encoded as $0.25$, $0.50$, and $0.75$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low (0.25)}: GenAI has limited autonomous ability on this task type and cannot reliably deliver satisfactory results without human intervention; it can replace only a small portion of work, but may still provide assistance under human supervision.
    \item \textbf{Medium (0.50)}: GenAI performs at a moderate level, completing parts of the task or offering useful recommendations, yet still requires human review and collaboration; it cannot independently replace human execution.
    \item \textbf{High (0.75)}: GenAI performs strongly and is close to professional human quality for many subtasks; under proper constraints and verification, it can handle a large share of the workflow and deliver significant augmentation, though it remains imperfect.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Note:} the values $0.25/0.50/0.75$ are coarse modeling encodings rather than precise measurements. All levels are grounded in publicly available reports and comparative experiments released within the last two years.

\subsubsection{Capability Evidence by Task Type (Narrative Justification)}

\paragraph{Code Generation}
\textbf{Substitution: High (0.75); Augmentation: High (0.75).}
GenAI has achieved strong performance in automated code generation. OpenAI GPT-4 reaches $67.0\%$ pass@1 on HumanEval, far above GPT-3.5 at $48.1\%$; Anthropic Claude 2 reaches $71.2\%$ on the same benchmark. This indicates that leading models solve more than half of typical coding problems correctly. Google CEO Sundar Pichai stated that roughly $25\%$ of new code at Google is generated by AI tools, reflecting substantial productivity gains: given intent or comments, models can produce large volumes of draft code and accelerate development. For simple modules, models can partially substitute manual coding. Nevertheless, outputs still require human review and testing to mitigate logic errors and security issues. Overall, coding/implementation is among the most substitution-prone and augmentation-rich domains for GenAI.

\paragraph{Testing and Verification}
\textbf{Substitution: Medium (0.50); Augmentation: High (0.75).}
GenAI shows moderate but improving capability for generating test cases and supporting verification. Studies report that LLM-generated unit tests can achieve coverage comparable to advanced automated tools and are often more readable. For instance, IBM Research experiments guided by static analysis found that GPT-based systems can generate Java/Python unit tests with competitive coverage and defect detection, while producing developer-friendly tests. This supports strong augmentation in routine function-level verification. However, generated tests may target irrelevant scenarios or weak assertions, requiring human review; thus full substitution remains difficult. In practice, GenAI can substantially reduce manual test-writing and speed up result checking, while humans validate test correctness.

\paragraph{Debugging and Bug Fixing}
\textbf{Substitution: Low (0.25); Augmentation: Medium (0.50).}
End-to-end debugging remains a weak area: autonomous success rates are relatively low, but assistant value is tangible. A 2025 Microsoft Research study reported that even with debugger tool access, the best model (Anthropic Claude 3.7 Sonnet) solved only $48.4\%$ of 300 curated debugging tasks on average, while others were around $30\%$. This suggests GenAI cannot fully replace human debugging. Limitations include ineffective use of debugging signals, poor strategy selection, and lack of stepwise reasoning traces. Therefore substitution is rated low. Meanwhile, augmentation is evident: with logs or exception traces, models can propose useful fixes; ``self-debugging'' via few-shot demonstrations improves success by about $2\%$--$12\%$ in some settings. In practice, GenAI functions more as an assistant than an independent mechanic.

\paragraph{System Design / Architecture}
\textbf{Substitution: Low (0.25); Augmentation: Medium (0.50).}
Architecture and system design demand global reasoning and responsible trade-offs; GenAI currently provides limited support. While no single authoritative benchmark directly measures system-design ability, models often produce plausible but incomplete plans, miss critical constraints, or rely on incorrect assumptions. Because system design lacks a single correct answer and requires accountable decision-making, autonomous substitution is rated low. Still, augmentation can be meaningful: models can draft design documents, summarize patterns, and brainstorm alternatives as a starting point for architects, improving discussion efficiency.

\paragraph{Requirements Analysis}
\textbf{Substitution: Low (0.25); Augmentation: Medium (0.50).}
Requirements work requires deep domain context and ambiguity resolution. GenAI can assist with summarizing and structuring text, but cannot replace analysts. Although dedicated evaluations are limited, related tasks such as NL-to-formal specifications provide evidence: on Spider (NL-to-SQL), even with enhancement techniques, GPT-4 gains are only about $2\%$--$3\%$, and complex queries remain challenging. This implies unreliable understanding for complex, ambiguous requirements, so substitution is low. However, augmentation is moderate: models can summarize lengthy documents, generate user-story templates, propose clarification questions, and accelerate organization of requirements under human supervision.

\paragraph{User Communication and Documentation}
\textbf{Substitution: Medium (0.50); Augmentation: High (0.75).}
GenAI excels in language generation, strongly augmenting user communication and technical documentation. Models such as GPT-4 and Claude 2 can produce coherent long-form drafts and adhere to structured formats (e.g., Markdown/JSON), enabling rapid drafting of API docs, manuals, and user responses. Many routine user inquiries can be answered fluently and consistently. Yet accuracy and hallucination risks remain, especially for system-specific details, so fully replacing human communication is still risky; substitution is rated medium while augmentation is rated high.

\subsection{Aggregating Task DNA into Occupation-Level Shock Parameters: Software Developers}

This section aggregates Software Developer Task DNA from task-level inputs $(w_{ij}, s_{ij}, c_{ij})$ into occupation-level shock parameters $(\alpha_i,\beta_i,\mu_i)$,
which will feed the evolution equation $\frac{dE_i(t)}{dt}=E_i(t)\big(g_i + A(t)(\beta_i-\alpha_i)\big)$.

\subsubsection{Top-$N$ Availability and Aggregation}

In practice, O*NET task ratings may be missing for some tasks.
We therefore adopt a ``Top-$N_i$ usable tasks'' strategy: for occupation $i$, we keep only tasks with sufficient ratings to construct weights, and renormalize so that $\sum_{j=1}^{N_i} w_{ij}=1$.
Occupation-level substitution pressure, augmentation gain, and net direction are defined as
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_i=\sum_{j=1}^{N_i} w_{ij} s_{ij},\qquad
    \beta_i=\sum_{j=1}^{N_i} w_{ij} c_{ij},\qquad
    \mu_i=\beta_i-\alpha_i.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Interpretability Decomposition: Top-$K$ Contribution Lower Bounds}

For interpretability, we decompose contributions from the top-weight tasks. Define partial sums
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_i^{(K)}=\sum_{j\in \text{Top-}K} w_{ij}s_{ij},\qquad
    \beta_i^{(K)}=\sum_{j\in \text{Top-}K} w_{ij}c_{ij}.
\end{equation}
For the currently available Top-5 sample, $\sum_{j\in \text{Top-}5} w_{ij}=0.360897$,
with partial contributions $\alpha_i^{(5)}=0.1804485$ and $\beta_i^{(5)}=0.2153186$,
indicating that high-weight tasks (often testing/maintenance/requirements/collaboration) will dominate the aggregate shocks.

\subsubsection{Validity Check of the Scoring Rule: Avoiding Near-Constant $s/c$}

If many tasks share the same discrete score (e.g., $0.50$), then $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ become weakly discriminative, undermining mechanistic explanation.
We therefore check weight-mass concentration over the discrete grid $\mathcal{G}=\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$:
\begin{equation}
    p_s(g)=\sum_{j:\,s_{ij}=g} w_{ij},\qquad
    p_c(g)=\sum_{j:\,c_{ij}=g} w_{ij},\qquad g\in\mathcal{G},
\end{equation}
using concentration and normalized entropy:
\begin{equation}
    C_s=\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}} p_s(g),\qquad
    C_c=\max_{g\in\mathcal{G}} p_c(g),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    H_s=-\frac{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}} p_s(g)\ln p_s(g)}{\ln|\mathcal{G}|},\qquad
    H_c=-\frac{\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}} p_c(g)\ln p_c(g)}{\ln|\mathcal{G}|}.
\end{equation}
High $C_s$ or low $H_s$ flags score compression and triggers a minimal calibration.

\subsubsection{Minimal Calibration: Category Defaults with Limited Manual Review}

To avoid re-collecting data, we calibrate only $s/c$ via a small set of task categories while keeping the task list and weights $w_{ij}$ unchanged.
Let $\kappa(j)$ map each task to a coarse category (e.g., requirements/stakeholders, coding/implementation, testing/validation/security, monitoring/operations) with a default $(s,c)$ pair.
We then manually review only a few top-weight tasks and record brief evidence notes, improving identifiability of $(\alpha_i,\beta_i)$ while preserving reproducibility and transparency.
