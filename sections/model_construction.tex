\subsection{Occupation Selection Across Three Domains}
For occupation selection, we do not treat this as an assumption but rather as an optimization problem. We use the heterogeneity among the three occupations as the objective function, aiming to ensure the model has maximum explanatory validity. We employ ``complete data support'' and ``policy relevance'' as constraints, ultimately ensuring that subsequent models are built upon a reproducible, data-driven framework.

\subsubsection{O*NET-Based Occupation Feature Parameters}
First, we need to position the three occupations along three feature dimensions to establish mechanistic contrast among them. The three domains specified by the problem naturally possess such conditions. We derive the feature parameters for the three occupations as $\textit{digit}(x)$, $\textit{physical}(x)$, and $\textit{iprisk}(x)$. We then score all U.S. occupations on these three dimensions. To avoid subjective assignment, we adopt the \textbf{O*NET} occupational database maintained by the U.S. Department of Labor~\cite{onet2025database} as the unified data source.
O*NET, based on expert surveys and practitioner feedback, provides numerous \emph{numerical scale descriptors} for each occupation, with most indicators naturally given as real numbers between $0$ and $100$, suitable for direct use in quantitative modeling.

We do not attempt to find a ``uniquely correct'' indicator for each dimension. For each feature axis, we select O*NET descriptors that semantically correspond directly to the three dimensions and are downloadable~\cite{onet2025database}, using them as proxy variables for that axis. We directly exclude occupations with missing descriptor fields.
Based on these constraints, we identify three dominant dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digitization degree $digit(x)$}: The extent to which core tasks can be digitally represented, automated, or augmented;
    \item \textbf{Physical anchoring $physical(x)$}: The degree of dependence on on-site physical presence or manual interaction with the environment for task execution;
    \item \textbf{Intellectual property and attribution risk $iprisk(x)$}: The sensitivity of value creation to authorship, originality, and ownership.
\end{itemize}

For reproducibility and auditability, we maintain the access links and descriptor IDs for the six descriptors in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:onet_descriptor_urls}.

Based on this, we filter all occupations that possess all six descriptor fields. For each occupation $x$, O*NET provides corresponding descriptor scores, so we let $v_m(x)$ denote its raw score under O*NET descriptor $m$ ($0$--$100$).
We apply min-max normalization across the entire occupation set:
\begin{equation}
    z_m(x)=\frac{v_m(x)-\min_{y}v_m(y)}{\max_{y}v_m(y)-\min_{y}v_m(y)} \in [0,1],
\end{equation}
where $y$ ranges over all occupations. Let the descriptor set for feature axis $k \in \{digit, physical, iprisk\}$ be $D_k$ (see Table~\ref{tab:onet_descriptor_mapping}), and we take the equal-weight average of multiple descriptors under the same feature axis:
\begin{equation}
    \textit{feature}_k(x) = \frac{1}{|D_k|} \sum_{m \in D_k} z_m(x).
\end{equation}
This yields the numerical values for $\textit{digit}(x)$, $\textit{physical}(x)$, and $\textit{iprisk}(x)$.

Each candidate occupation $x$ is represented by a normalized three-dimensional feature vector, serving as a data point in a 3D scatter plot.
\begin{equation}
    f(x)=\big(digit(x),\,physical(x),\,iprisk(x)\big)\in[0,1]^3,
\end{equation}
To select three occupations that constitute maximum heterogeneity (diversity) among qualifying occupations, we approach this geometrically: finding three data points that form the largest triangular plane in the three-dimensional coordinate system. Following the standard area formula:

\begin{equation}
Area3D(x_S, x_T, x_A) = \frac{1}{2} \left\lVert \big(f(x_T) - f(x_S)\big) \times \big(f(x_A) - f(x_S)\big) \right\rVert_{2},
\label{eq:area3d}
\end{equation}
where $x_S \in \text{STEM}$, $x_T \in \text{Trade}$, $x_A \in \text{Arts}$.

We then visualize using Python (Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity3d} and Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity2d}).

\subsection{Model Construction for Occupational Evolution}

The second core requirement of the problem is to construct a \emph{data-driven} future evolution model for the three selected occupations, emphasizing the combination of ``current trajectory'' and related driving factors.
First, from first principles, an occupation is defined by its task composition (DNA); therefore, under Gen-AI's influence, its future Task DNA will inevitably undergo structural ``mutation.'' Thus, attempting to predict future job demand for that occupation is logically problematic, as the occupation at that time will no longer be the same as today.

Therefore, we do not directly predict future occupational job demand, but instead approach from a more fundamental angle, studying the structural changes in occupational Task DNA under the current Gen-AI development trajectory.

For ease of understanding, we draw Figure~\ref{fig:task_redefinition} to illustrate the occupational evolution process.

\subsubsection{Task-Level Dynamic Prediction Model}

Since the official raw task description fields for occupations are too numerous, filtering is required. By constructing Task DNA task weights $w_{ij}$, combined with authoritative data sources, we calculate substitution/augmentation intensities $(s_{ij},c_{ij})$ at the task (rather than occupation) level, obtaining the task-level net impact for the occupation as
\begin{equation}
    \mu_{ij}^{task}=c_{ij}-s_{ij},
\end{equation}
and define ``current trajectory'' as society's AI adoption rate $A(t)\in[0,1]$, which drives the evolution of task demand over time.

Furthermore, we begin constructing the mathematical model for task-level prediction.

For occupation $i$, we select its \textbf{top N core tasks} as its Task DNA (task index $j = 1, \ldots, 20$). Each task $j$ is taken from O*NET's task description $Task_j$ for this occupation~\cite{onet2025database}. The database provides importance and frequency scoring data for each occupation's task composition, so we define two descriptors based on this data:
\textbf{Task Importance} $IM_j$ (range 0--100);
\textbf{Task Frequency} $FR_j$ (range 0--100, or equivalent frequency scale).

We define task weight as the normalized value of the product of importance $IM_j$ and frequency $FR_j$. Let the unnormalized weight be:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{w}_j = IM_j \cdot FR_j,
\end{equation}
then normalize:
\begin{equation}
    w_{ij} = \frac{\tilde{w}_j}{\sum_{k=1}^{20} \tilde{w}_k}, \quad \sum_{j=1}^{20} w_j = 1.
\label{eq:task_weight_norm}
\end{equation}

Based on Equation~\eqref{eq:task_weight_norm}, we compute each occupation's task weights using Python programming (if the selected SOC code lacks the task frequency scale $FR_j$, we use only importance values, i.e., $\tilde{w}_j = IM_j$), then select the Top Tasks (up to 20) for the three occupations and visualize them. Distribution results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:task_dna_imfr_triptych}; task text legends are in Appendix~\ref{app:task_dna_figures}.

Combining the Task DNA of the three occupation types, we provide targeted interpretations: for \textbf{Robotics Engineers}, occupational value is primarily driven by a few high-weight technical tasks; for \textbf{First-Line Supervisors (Mechanics/Installation/Repair)}, points are more dispersed with higher proportions of medium-weight tasks, reflecting the coexistence of management, coordination, and on-site judgment tasks, with structural change contributed by multiple tasks; while \textbf{Court Reporting and Captioning} highly depends on a few core language processing tasks.

After obtaining the Top Tasks for the three occupations, we need to map them to current Gen-AI capabilities to study the impact intensity on each Task. Thus, we similarly need to dissect current Gen-AI capabilities, dividing them into multiple capability dimensions. The following three points serve as the main basis for division:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Coverage of major work activity types: from technical problem-solving to language processing, encompassing the main categories of occupational tasks
    \item Quantifiable and auditable: each dimension has corresponding authoritative benchmark tests, avoiding subjective evaluation
    \item Matching the task composition of the current three occupations: each Task DNA can be measured through publicly available AI test scores.
\end{itemize}

To avoid subjective continuous scoring, we construct a horizontal discrete capability scale for capability segmentation ($M_k$):
\begin{itemize}[label={},leftmargin=*]
    \item[] High ($\ge 0.85$)\quad
    Moderately High ($0.70$--$0.85$)\quad
    Medium ($0.50$--$0.70$)\quad
    Low ($0.30$--$0.50$)\quad
    Very Low ($<0.30$)
\end{itemize}
with representative normalized scores $M_k$ taken within each interval (specific mapping in Table~\ref{tab:genai_scale}).

We also introduce the {direction coefficient ($\rho_k$)} to characterize the bias strength of ``substitution/complementarity/balance'': if
$\rho_k<0$: substitution-biased (AI can independently complete deliverables);
if $\rho_k>0$: complementarity-biased (AI provides suggestions/assistance, human must be in the loop);
if $\rho_k=0$: balanced (both strong, net effect uncertain).
$\rho_k = c_k - s_k$ represents the direction of GenAI's impact on that dimension: positive for complementarity (improving human efficiency increases demand), negative for substitution (automation decreases demand).

\subsubsection{Coupling ``Development Trajectory'' with ``Task Demand''}

The problem requires ``given the current trajectory and expected impacts of Gen-AI,'' where we define ``current trajectory'' as an exogenous adoption path $A(t)\in[0,1]$ representing Gen-AI's adoption rate at time $t$. Furthermore, 0 is treated as the current time point, meaning the span from present to future. To ensure this exogenous trajectory's magnitude is consistent with reality, we reference trend boundaries and scenario upper bounds from organizational-level AI adoption surveys and industry forecasts~\cite{mckinsey2024stateai,mckinsey2025stateai,gartner2023genai}.

Considering model applicability and accuracy, we do not make any quantitative predictions for any future time point, but instead approach from a more fundamental angle, exploring changes in future task relative shares. We introduce several indicators to determine ``the essential nature of tasks has changed.''
\paragraph{(Indicator I) Structural Change Contribution Rate}
First, define the unnormalized task weight

\begin{equation}
    q_{ij}(t)=w_{ij}\Big[(1-A(t))+A(t)\exp\big(\eta\,\mu^{task}_{ij}\big)\Big],
\end{equation}
where $\eta>0$ is the impact intensity scaling (dimensionless), characterizing ``the magnitude of task structure rearrangement brought by AI adoption rate,'' with its effects reported in sensitivity analysis.

Intuitively, when $A(t)=0$, only $w_{ij}$ remains, keeping task weights consistent with current state; as $A(t)$ increases, since the right-side portion applies a multiplicative transformation monotonically consistent with $\mu^{task}_{ij}$ (ensuring positive weights), the task-level net impact $\mu^{task}_{ij}$ gradually releases, and task demand structure rearranges accordingly.


Then normalizing $q_{ij}(t)$ yields the relative task share.
\begin{equation}
    p_{ij}(t)=\frac{q_{ij}(t)}{\sum_{k=1}^{J_i} q_{ik}(t)}.
\end{equation}

When $A(t)$ rises (GenAI adoption rate increases), if $\mu^{task}_{ij}<0$, then $q_{ij}(t)$ relatively shrinks and $p_{ij}(t)$ decreases; conversely if $\mu^{task}_{ij}>0$, that task's share rises.

For any two time points $t_0,t_1$, task $j$'s share change is defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:delta_p}
r_{ij}(t_0,t_1)=\left|\Delta p_{ij}(t_0,t_1)\right|=p_{ij}(t_1)-p_{ij}(t_0).
\end{equation}

Sorting by $r_{ij}$ in descending order identifies tasks contributing most to structural change (top contributors).
\paragraph{(Indicator II) Top-$m$ Turnover Rate}

Define an occupation's core task set as the top $m$ tasks sorted by $p_{ij}(t)$ in descending order, denoted $\mathrm{Top}_m(p_i(t))$. The turnover rate measures the degree of change in the core task set:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:turnover}
J_i(t_0,t_1;m)=1-\frac{\left|\mathrm{Top}_m(p_i(t_0))\cap \mathrm{Top}_m(p_i(t_1))\right|}{m}.
\end{equation}
When $J_i=0$, the core task set is completely unchanged; when $J_i=1$, the core task set is completely replaced.

\paragraph{(Indicator III) Rank Inversion and Normalized Inversion Intensity}
Let $\mathrm{rank}_i(j,t)$ denote task $j$'s rank in occupation $i$ sorted by $p_{ij}(t)$ in descending order (1 being highest). Define the inversion count as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:inversion_count}
I_i(t_0,t_1)=\#\{(a,b):\ \mathrm{rank}_i(a,t_0)<\mathrm{rank}_i(b,t_0)\ \wedge\ \mathrm{rank}_i(a,t_1)>\mathrm{rank}_i(b,t_1)\}.
\end{equation}
This indicator counts the number of task pairs where ``at $t_0$, $a$ ranked ahead of $b$, but at $t_1$, $a$ fell behind $b$.'' For cross-occupation comparison, we normalize:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:normalized_inversion}
\widetilde I_i(t_0,t_1)=\frac{2I_i(t_0,t_1)}{N_i(N_i-1)}\in[0,1],
\end{equation}
where $N_i$ is the total number of tasks in occupation $i$. $\widetilde I_i$, as the normalized form of Kendall $\tau$ distance~\cite{kendall1938rank}, explains the degree of structural change in task relative importance.
The above indicators serve as the main criteria for ``occupation has been redefined'':
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:redefinition_trigger}
\text{Structure Redefined} \Longleftrightarrow \widetilde I_i(t_0,t_1) \ge \theta_{I} \quad \text{or} \quad J_i(t_0,t_1;m) \ge \theta_{J},
\end{equation}
where $\theta_{I}\in(0,1)$ is the inversion intensity threshold (e.g., $\theta_{I}=0.15$), $\theta_{J}\in(0,1)$ is the turnover rate threshold (e.g., $\theta_{J}=0.40$), and $m$ is the number of core tasks (e.g., $m=5$).

Thresholds $\theta_{I}$ and $\theta_{J}$ are not fixed constants; we compute results under multiple threshold settings and present optimal results.

\subsubsection{Exploring the Future of the Three Selected Occupations}

Based on the task prediction model, GenAI capability mapping (Table~\ref{tab:genai_scale}), and the structural change indicators defined above, we now comprehensively analyze results and explore each occupation's future trajectory under the GenAI adoption path.

Our analysis indicates that Robotics Engineers exhibit a balanced but augmentation-leaning impact profile. Figure~\ref{fig:task_dna_sc_triptych}a shows complementarity scores $c$ concentrated in mid-to-high tiers, while approximately 40\% of task weight falls in the negative net impact region, primarily concentrated in dimension D3 (Algorithm Programming \& Automation), with $\mu = -0.20$. However, this portion of substitution pressure is offset by strong augmentation effects in engineering problem-solving (D1) and data analysis (D2). This ``give-and-take'' symmetric pattern indicates: GenAI is not simply ``eliminating'' certain task types, but triggering task weight rebalancing within the occupation.

Under the baseline scenario with $\eta = 1.0$, reconfiguration mass grows from $R(t_0) = 0.086$ to $R(t_{\text{end}}) = 0.121$, indicating approximately 12\% cumulative structural transformation when adoption rate saturates at $A = 0.95$. Top-5 turnover rate stabilizes at $J_5 = 0.40$, meaning two of the five most important tasks are replaced by previously secondary tasks. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rank_flip_stem}, rank inversion patterns indicate declining relative shares for routine coding and documentation tasks, while system-level integration, hardware-software co-design, and cross-functional collaboration tasks rise.

We conclude that future robotics engineers will primarily be responsible for orchestrating AI-assisted development workflows, validating machine-generated solutions, and focusing on tasks requiring physical-world judgment and creative problem-solving---capabilities current GenAI cannot replicate.

For First-Line Supervisors of Mechanics, Installation, and Repair, this occupation exhibits the lowest substitution risk among the three. The $(s,c)$ distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:task_dna_sc_triptych}b) is notably dispersed, with no single dimension dominating the impact profile. Only one task (scheduling and dispatch, task\_id = 2912) has negative net impact, contributing only 0.059 of total task weight. Dominant dimensions---D4 (On-Site Perception \& Physical Operation), D6 (Collaboration \& Training), and D7 (Supervision \& Quality Control)---all exhibit positive or neutral $\rho_k$ values, indicating GenAI's complementary rather than substitutive effects.

Under the baseline scenario ($\eta = 1.0$), reconfiguration mass grows modestly from $R(t_0) = 0.061$ to $R(t_{\text{end}}) = 0.086$, the smallest structural change among the three occupations. However, Top-5 turnover rate reaches $J_5 = 0.80$, which appears contradictory at first glance. This high turnover rate reflects the flat weight distribution characteristic of supervisory roles: many similarly important tasks compete for Top-5 positions, making rankings sensitive to minor perturbations even with limited total reconfiguration.

Future first-line supervisors will remain structurally stable but operationally enhanced. GenAI tools will enhance diagnostic capabilities, streamline scheduling processes, and improve training delivery, but the core value proposition---real-time judgment in unpredictable physical environments, interpersonal coordination, and hands-on troubleshooting---remains firmly human-anchored. This occupation embodies the ``AI as copilot'' paradigm rather than transformation or substitution.

\paragraph{Court Reporters and Simultaneous Captioners (27-3092.00): Substitution-Dominant Disruption}

Court reporters face the most severe substitution pressure among the three occupations. The $(s,c)$ distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:task_dna_sc_triptych}c) shows high-weight tasks clearly concentrated in dimension D8 (Language Understanding \& Text Generation), where $s = 0.90$ and $c = 0.50$ produce strong negative net impact $\mu = -0.40$. The cumulative weight of negative-impact tasks reaches $\sum_{j:\mu_{ij}<0} w_{ij} = 0.413$, the highest among the three occupations.

Under the baseline scenario ($\eta = 1.0$), reconfiguration mass grows from $R(t_0) = 0.060$ to $R(t_{\text{end}}) = 0.092$. While the absolute magnitude appears moderate, the direction of change is unambiguous: core transcription and drafting task shares decline rapidly, while the relative importance of quality verification, legal context interpretation, and certification tasks rises. The $J_5 = 0.60$ Top-5 turnover rate confirms substantial restructuring of the task hierarchy.

Future court reporters will experience fundamental role redefinition. As ASR and GenAI transcription tools achieve near-human accuracy in controlled acoustic environments, the occupation shifts from stenographic production toward quality assurance and legal certification. Practitioners will increasingly serve as expert validators, ensuring transcription accuracy in adversarial legal environments, interpreting domain-specific terminology, and maintaining chain of custody for official records. Those who fail to acquire complementary skills in legal technology, quality management, or specialized domains will face significant substitution risk.
