\subsection{Educational Programs and Gen-AI Integration Strategies}

\label{subsec:task3_programs}

Based on the aforementioned occupation selection and task-level Gen-AI impact analysis, we recommend one representative educational program for each of the three occupations. We follow three criteria: \textbf{whether the institution demonstrates strong industry leadership in the relevant sector}, \textbf{whether the curriculum aligns well with industry demands}, and \textbf{whether the institution already possesses Gen-AI-related resources}. Specific selections and rationale are provided in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:programs_genai_rationale}.

For \textbf{Robotics Engineers}, we recommend Carnegie Mellon University's Robotics Institute MRSD program, which possesses industry leadership and has established Gen-AI-related curriculum resources.
For \textbf{First-Line Supervisors of Mechanics}, Universal Technical Institute (UTI) demonstrates the ability to rapidly respond to industry technology transitions and has already incorporated AI diagnostics into its educational narrative.
For \textbf{Court Reporters}, Generations College, as the historical inheritor of America's first court reporting program, offers a Realtime training system that complements Gen-AI era editing tools.

\subsubsection{Meta-Model: Employment--Humanistic--AI Overlap}
\label{subsubsec:three_axis_metamodel}

After determining the recommended programs, we construct a \textbf{curriculum-level evaluation framework} to guide course reform recommendations within each program. The model's core idea is: each course is evaluated along three orthogonal dimensions---\emph{employability}, \emph{humanistic value}, and \emph{AI overlap}---thereby yielding three directional choices: ``Keep / Transform / Prune.'' The employment axis focuses more on labor market demands; the humanistic axis is primarily subjectively defined; the AI overlap axis focuses solely on the connection between course content and Gen-AI capability dimensions.

Let the educational program contain $M$ courses, with course index $m=1,\ldots,M$, and course text description $t_m$ (course title and description). The target occupation set is denoted $O$, with occupation $o\in O$ corresponding to the O*NET task database. Define:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w_o$: Labor market demand weight for occupation $o$ (set to uniform distribution if unavailable);
    \item $v_{o,j}$: Saliency weight of task $j$ in occupation $o$, obtained from O*NET importance$\times$frequency normalization; this is the method already implemented in Question 2.
    \item $s_{m,o,j}=\mathrm{sim}(t_m,\mathrm{task}_{o,j})$: Semantic coverage of course $m$ for task $(o,j)$ (based on text similarity);
    \item $b_k$: Humanistic dimension anchor text, $k\in\{\mathrm{civic,agency,wellbeing,lifelong}\}$;
    \item $k=1,\ldots,9$: The aforementioned nine-dimensional Gen-AI capability dimension index;
    \item $w_{m,k}\in[0,1]$: Normalized footprint of course $m$ in dimension $k$, $\sum_{k=1}^9 w_{m,k}=1$ (extracted from course title and description);
    \item $(M_k,s_k,c_k,\rho_k)$: Nine-dimensional capability scale parameters, where $\rho_k=c_k-s_k$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(Axis I) Employability}
The core question of the employment axis is: what is the course's \emph{marginal contribution} to students entering the target occupation set $O$? We use ``occupation-demand-weighted task coverage rate'' to characterize the program's match with the labor market.

First, define the program's coverage of task $(o,j)$ as the maximum similarity across all courses:
\begin{equation}
    c_{o,j}=\max_{m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}} s_{m,o,j}.
\end{equation}
The overall employability score is:
\begin{equation}
    E=\sum_{o\in O} w_o \cdot \sum_{j\in J(o)} v_{o,j}\cdot c_{o,j}.
\end{equation}
To measure a single course's contribution, we define \textbf{marginal employment value}. Let $E^{(-m)}$ denote the employability score after removing course $m$:
\begin{equation}
    E^{(-m)}=\sum_{o\in O} w_o \cdot \sum_{j\in J(o)} v_{o,j}\cdot \max_{n\neq m} s_{n,o,j}.
\end{equation}
Then course $m$'s marginal contribution is:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta E_m=E-E^{(-m)}.
\end{equation}
The interpretable meaning of $\Delta E_m$ is: if course $m$ is removed, how much will the program's alignment with the current labor market decrease; the larger $\Delta E_m$, the more \emph{irreplaceable} the course is on the employment axis.

\paragraph{(Axis II) Humanistic Value}
The humanistic axis focuses on a course's cultivation of students' \emph{non-occupational capabilities}. We use anchor text similarity as a concise and reproducible metric:
\begin{equation}
    H_m=\sum_{k\in\{\mathrm{civic,agency,wellbeing,lifelong}\}} \mathrm{sim}(t_m, b_k).
\end{equation}
where $b_k$ are predefined humanistic anchor texts. The four dimensions and their anchor keywords are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Civic Literacy}: \texttt{ethics}, \texttt{public}, \texttt{civic}, \texttt{law}, \texttt{compliance}, \texttt{responsibility};
    \item \textbf{Agency}: \texttt{decision}, \texttt{leadership}, \texttt{management}, \texttt{planning}, \texttt{strategy}, \texttt{autonomy};
    \item \textbf{Well-being}: \texttt{safety}, \texttt{health}, \texttt{wellbeing}, \texttt{risk}, \texttt{care}, \texttt{quality};
    \item \textbf{Lifelong Learning}: \texttt{learning}, \texttt{training}, \texttt{skill}, \texttt{development}, \texttt{professional}, \texttt{continuous}.
\end{itemize}
This axis identifies courses that, while not directly improving short-term employment matching, contribute to students' long-term development and social public value.

\paragraph{(Axis III) AI Overlap}
The AI overlap axis measures the \emph{substitution pressure} between course content and Gen-AI capability dimensions. Unlike approaches based on keywords or opaque dictionaries, we define the AI-Overlap metric as the weighted overlap between ``course nine-dimensional capability footprint'' and ``Gen-AI's independent delivery strength in each dimension,'' fully reusing the aforementioned nine-dimensional capability scale parameters $(M_k,s_k,c_k,\rho_k)$, thereby avoiding subjective weighting.

First, define dimension gating (two calibers):

The only difference lies in the domain; using 1 to avoid affecting numerical values.
\begin{equation}
    I_k=\mathbf{1}[\rho_k\le 0], \qquad I_k^{\mathrm{strict}}=\mathbf{1}[\rho_k<0].
\end{equation}
where $I_k$ allows ``uncertain/balanced'' dimensions to enter the pressure measure, while $I_k^{\mathrm{strict}}$ only counts ``explicitly substitution-oriented'' dimensions into pressure, used to construct conservative pruning conditions. Define dimension-level substitutability intensity as:
\begin{equation}
    a_k=M_k\cdot s_k\cdot I_k, \qquad a_k^{\mathrm{strict}}=M_k\cdot s_k\cdot I_k^{\mathrm{strict}}.
\end{equation}
Then course $m$'s AI-Overlap (two calibers) is:
\begin{equation}
    A_m=\sum_{k=1}^{9} w_{m,k}\,a_k, \qquad A_m^{\mathrm{strict}}=\sum_{k=1}^{9} w_{m,k}\,a_k^{\mathrm{strict}}.
\end{equation}
$A_m$ reflects the overall overlap pressure between the course and Gen-AI capability dimensions, while $A_m^{\mathrm{strict}}$ reflects the conservative pressure from ``proven substitution'' dimensions. This definition maintains consistency with the capability dimension research in the previous two questions and depends only on course text (title and description).

\paragraph{Three-Axis Decision Rules: Keep / Transform / Prune}
Based on the three-axis metrics, we provide course decision rules. The intuitive logic is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prune}: The course simultaneously satisfies ``low employment margin,'' ``low humanistic value,'' and ``extremely high strict AI overlap.'' Such courses lack both employment and humanistic support, and their core outputs are highly deliverable independently by Gen-AI; we recommend removing them from the curriculum.
    \item \textbf{Transform}: The course has high AI overlap but does not satisfy the conservative pruning conditions. Such courses should not be deleted but redesigned (e.g., compressing outputs that can be directly generated by Gen-AI, shifting toward human-led process evidence, on-site reasoning, ethical deliberation, and human-AI collaboration capabilities).
    \item \textbf{Keep}: Remaining courses maintain their current form.
\end{itemize}

To ensure cross-institutional portability and avoid fabricating constants, we define thresholds as empirical quantiles within the program (using credit-weighted quantiles when course credits are available; otherwise, uniform quantiles). Let
\begin{equation}
    e_{\mathrm{low}}=Q_{0.25}(\Delta E_m),\qquad
    h_{\mathrm{low}}=Q_{0.25}(H_m),\qquad
    a_{\mathrm{high}}=Q_{0.75}(A_m),\qquad
    a_{\mathrm{strict\_high}}=Q_{0.90}(A_m^{\mathrm{strict}}).
\end{equation}
The final decision rules are:
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{PRUNE}(m) &= \mathbf{1}\Big[(\Delta E_m\le e_{\mathrm{low}})\land(H_m\le h_{\mathrm{low}})\land(A_m^{\mathrm{strict}}\ge a_{\mathrm{strict\_high}})\Big],\\
    \mathrm{TRANSFORM}(m) &= \mathbf{1}\Big[\neg \mathrm{PRUNE}(m)\land(A_m\ge a_{\mathrm{high}})\Big],\\
    \mathrm{KEEP}(m) &= 1-\mathrm{PRUNE}(m)-\mathrm{TRANSFORM}(m).
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Institution-Level Strategic Recommendations}
\label{subsubsec:institutional_recommendations}

Based on the results from the above meta-model, it is noteworthy that the prune lists for all three programs are empty, with all recommendations falling under ``Transform'' rather than ``Prune.'' This result aligns well with reality: educational reform proceeds incrementally rather than through unrealistic wholesale elimination. We further provide targeted Gen-AI integration strategy recommendations for the leaders of the three institutions.

\paragraph{Generations College: Court Reporting Program}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item \textbf{Redefine core competencies: From ``speed'' to ``judgment''}. Traditional court reporting emphasizes real-time transcription speeds above 225 words per minute, while Gen-AI's advancing speech recognition capabilities are changing the boundaries of ``transferable skills'' that students need to train. We recommend shifting the curriculum focus from ``mechanical speed training'' to ``real-time verification and semantic judgment''---cultivating students' ability to perform legal terminology correction, speaker identification, and courtroom context understanding on AI drafts~\citep{mughal2025beyondautomationho}.
    \item \textbf{Introduce human-AI collaboration workflows}. The curriculum should design AI-assisted stenography experimental modules, allowing students to perform human intervention on AI real-time transcription outputs, annotate uncertain passages, and conduct attribution analysis of AI errors. This not only enhances students' awareness of AI limitations but also lays the foundation for human-AI collaboration in their future careers.
    \item \textbf{Strengthen legal ethics and responsibility assessment}. Court records have legal effect, and responsibility for AI-generated content accuracy ultimately rests with humans. The curriculum should add an ``AI output auditing'' unit to cultivate students' ability to identify AI hallucination, bias, and privacy leakage risks.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Carnegie Mellon University: MRSD Program}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item \textbf{From ``algorithm implementation'' to ``system integration and boundary conditions''}. Gen-AI can already generate high-quality control algorithm code and optimization solvers. We recommend that 16-642 \textit{Manipulation, Estimation \& Control} compress traditional algorithm derivation and coding training, shifting emphasis toward: (a) sensor noise, actuator delay, and environmental uncertainty in real physical systems; (b) safety auditing and boundary condition testing of AI-generated code; (c) fault recovery and ethical decision-making in human-AI collaboration scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Business courses focus on ``non-automatable'' strategic judgment}. The two business courses 16-697 and 16-698 should reduce dependence on content that Gen-AI can rapidly generate, such as market analysis reports and financial models, shifting emphasis toward: (a) strategic selection and timing judgment of technical roadmaps; (b) cross-cultural team management and stakeholder negotiation; (c) case studies on technology ethics and social responsibility.
    \item \textbf{Establish ``AI literacy'' horizontal modules}. Given that MRSD students will lead robotics R\&D teams in the future, we recommend introducing AI literacy required modules at the program level, covering: capability boundaries of large language models, prompt engineering best practices, and explainability and auditing methods for AI systems; this aligns with higher education evidence on ``elevating AI capabilities from individual experimentation to institutionalized curriculum design and policy frameworks''~\citep{jogezai2025generativeartifici,wong2025thefutureoflearnin}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Universal Technical Institute: Diesel Technology Program}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\roman*.]
    \item \textbf{Incorporate AI diagnostic tools into hands-on training}. Modern diesel vehicles are equipped with OBD-II diagnostic interfaces and AI fault prediction systems. We recommend adding an ``AI diagnostic tools hands-on'' module to the curriculum, allowing students to learn how to interpret AI-generated fault codes, evaluate the reliability of AI recommendations, and make physical inspection and repair decisions based on AI prompts.
    \item \textbf{Strengthen physical skills that ``AI cannot replace''}. Although AI can assist diagnosis, actual disassembly, calibration, and repair still require human technicians. The curriculum should clearly distinguish between ``AI-assistable'' and ``AI-irreplaceable'' skill boundaries, and increase hands-on practical weighting in assessments while reducing dependence on theoretical knowledge memorization.
    \item \textbf{Introduce electrification and hybrid powertrain transition content}. As the transportation industry transitions toward electrification, market demand for traditional diesel technology will gradually shrink. We recommend adding electric and hybrid system modules to the curriculum, helping students build adaptability across technology platforms and reducing career risk from single technology paths.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Cross-Institutional Common Recommendations}

Beyond the institution-specific recommendations above, we propose three cross-institutional common principles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assessment system transformation: From ``output'' to ``process''}. Traditional assessment focuses on students' final submitted work (papers, code, reports), while Gen-AI makes ``automatic generation'' of these outputs possible. We recommend shifting assessment focus toward process evidence---such as live oral defenses, real-time problem-solving, and collaboration process documentation---to ensure assessment validity and fairness~\citep{harris2024processoverproduct}.
    \item \textbf{Faculty development first}. The prerequisite for curriculum transformation is faculty possessing Gen-AI literacy. Institutions should prioritize investment in faculty training, enabling them to understand Gen-AI capability boundaries, master human-AI collaborative instructional design methods, and demonstrate responsible AI use in the classroom~\citep{jogezai2025generativeartifici}.
    \item \textbf{Continuous monitoring and iteration}. Gen-AI capabilities are rapidly evolving, and currently ``irreplaceable'' dimensions may be breached in the future. We recommend institutions establish annual curriculum audit mechanisms, periodically re-evaluate three-axis metrics, and dynamically adjust curriculum design according to Gen-AI capability evolution; meanwhile, clear institutionalized policies on ``allowing use but setting boundaries'' are needed to reduce the learning and integrity tensions from gray areas~\citep{hamerman2024aninvestigationofg,hashemi2025navigatingthedoubl}.
\end{itemize}
